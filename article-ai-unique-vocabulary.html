The Need for an AI-Unique Vocabulary

Designing Language for Humans and AI

As AI becomes an active participant in understanding requirements, generating specifications, retrieving knowledge, writing code, and making decisions, we need to rethink something very fundamental: the words we use to describe our systems.

Traditionally, naming was primarily designed for humans. A name only needed to make sense to people who understood the surrounding context.

That assumption is changing.

Today, the same language is consumed by humans, AI assistants, RAG systems, semantic search engines, embeddings, agents, documentation generators, and coding assistants. When the terminology we use is generic or overloaded, the surrounding context may not always be sufficient to establish exactly what we mean.

This creates a new requirement:

Important concepts within a system should have distinctive, stable, and semantically unambiguous names that both humans and AI can recognize.

1. The Problem: Common Words Carry Too Many Meanings

Consider words such as Platform or Application.

Within one enterprise, we might have:

  • AI Platform
  • AI Application
  • Cloud Platform
  • Data Platform
  • Developer Platform
  • Integration Platform
  • Application Platform
  • Business Platform

For a human reading an entire document, the meaning may be reasonably obvious.

But AI often does not receive the entire document.

A RAG system may retrieve only a few chunks. An agent may receive a summarized context. A coding assistant may see only the files relevant to a particular task. Semantic retrieval may identify several passages containing similar concepts.

Suppose the documentation says:

"The Platform validates the request before sending it to the service."

Which platform?

The original author may know. A team member may infer it from the document. But once this sentence is extracted from its original location and placed into an AI context window, much of that implicit context disappears.

The problem therefore is not merely terminology.

It is context portability.

A good term should retain its identity even when it travels outside its original paragraph, document, application, or conversation.

2. Similar Names Can Create Semantic Collisions

Imagine that a solution contains two concepts:

  • Customer Data Platform — A platform responsible for ingesting and managing customer information.
  • Customer Engagement Platform — A platform responsible for customer campaigns and interactions.

Documentation may gradually start shortening both:

  • "Customer Platform"
  • "Platform service"
  • "Customer service"
  • "Engagement service"

For humans working on the project every day, these shortcuts may remain understandable.

For semantic retrieval, however, the terms customer, platform, and service occur throughout both domains.

A question such as:

"How does the Customer Platform authorize a request?"

could therefore retrieve content from both systems.

Now imagine that the first concept instead has a distinctive project vocabulary:

  • CustVault = customer-data management domain
  • EngageSphere = customer-engagement domain

Documentation can then say:

"CustVault validates ownership before exposing customer information."

and

"EngageSphere uses customer segments to initiate a campaign."

Even if these sentences are extracted independently, their identities survive.

The distinctive term acts almost like a semantic namespace.

3. The Same Principle Applies to Processes

This concept should not be limited to components.

Consider an insurance system containing several review processes:

  • Claim Review
  • Policy Review
  • Risk Review
  • Compliance Review

Documentation may repeatedly contain sentences such as:

"The review process sends the case for approval."

If that sentence is retrieved independently, its meaning is weak.

Instead, important processes can receive distinctive identities:

  • ClaimLens = claim assessment process
  • PolicyGate = policy validation process
  • RiskProbe = risk evaluation process
  • ComplyCheck = regulatory compliance process

Now documentation can state:

"ClaimLens sends claims above the defined threshold to manual assessment."

That statement carries considerably more context by itself.

4. It Becomes Even More Important for AI Agents

Consider an enterprise AI system containing agents for:

  • requirement analysis
  • architecture generation
  • code generation
  • security review
  • testing
  • deployment

If every document simply refers to "the agent", ambiguity quickly develops.

Instead, establish identities such as:

  • SpecScout = requirement-analysis agent
  • ArchPilot = architecture agent
  • CodeForge = code-generation agent
  • SecureLens = security-review agent
  • TestProbe = testing agent

Then instructions become naturally self-identifying:

"SpecScout converts the business requirement into structured specifications."

"ArchPilot consumes the SpecScout output."

"SecureLens evaluates the ArchPilot design before CodeForge starts implementation."

The vocabulary itself now communicates part of the architecture.

Humans can understand the flow, but so can an AI system attempting to retrieve, summarize, reason about, or execute it.

5. A Business Example

The same problem exists outside technology.

Imagine a banking organization with:

  • Customer Onboarding
  • Partner Onboarding
  • Employee Onboarding
  • Merchant Onboarding

Hundreds of documents may therefore contain the word onboarding.

Someone asks an AI assistant:

"What approvals are required during onboarding?"

The AI must determine which onboarding process was intended.

Instead, the organization could define durable identities:

  • ClientStart = customer onboarding
  • PartnerLink = partner onboarding
  • MerchantLaunch = merchant onboarding

A business requirement can then say:

"MerchantLaunch requires fraud verification before account activation."

That statement remains understandable even when extracted from a much larger specification.

This is important because the consumer of enterprise documentation is no longer only the employee who wrote it.

The consumer may also be an AI system encountering one paragraph among millions.

6. Think of These Terms as Semantic Anchors

The purpose is not to invent unusual words simply for creativity.

A useful term should be a Semantic Anchor.

A Semantic Anchor is a stable and distinctive word or phrase assigned to an important:

  • component
  • process
  • capability
  • business concept
  • data object
  • workflow
  • agent
  • control
  • architectural boundary

Once established, that term should be used consistently across:

Business Requirement → Functional Specification → Architecture → API → Code → Test Cases → Operational Documentation → AI Knowledge Base

For example:

RiskProbe

  • Business requirement: RiskProbe evaluates applications before credit approval.
  • Architecture: RiskProbe receives applicant data through the decision API.
  • Code: RiskProbeService
  • Test: RiskProbe_HighRiskApplicant_RequiresManualReview
  • Monitoring: RiskProbeDecisionLatency
  • Runbook: If RiskProbe becomes unavailable, applications enter the manual-review queue.

The terminology now becomes a thread connecting the entire system.

7. The Important Principle: Uniqueness Matters More Than Length

A common naming practice is to create increasingly descriptive names:

Enterprise Customer Identity Verification Platform

This appears precise, but most of its individual words are generic.

Different systems may contain:

  • Enterprise Identity Platform
  • Customer Identity Service
  • Identity Verification Service
  • Customer Verification Platform

Their semantic neighborhoods remain extremely close.

A distinctive identity such as TrustID, for example, may be much easier to retrieve precisely when it has been formally defined.

A practical pattern is:

Unique Identity + Human Description

For example:

  • TrustID – Customer Identity Verification
  • FlowGate – Transaction Approval Workflow
  • DataHarbor – Governed Enterprise Data Repository
  • PolicyLens – Policy Evaluation Engine

The human-readable description explains the concept.

The distinctive identity preserves it.

8. Do Not Completely Eliminate Common Words

Words such as platform, service, workflow, customer, cloud, data, and agent remain useful.

The objective is not to ban them.

Instead of writing:

"The platform sends information to the data service."

write:

"AstraHub, the AI orchestration platform, sends the request to DataHarbor."

After the terms are established, subsequent documentation can simply say:

"AstraHub sends the request to DataHarbor."

Specific identity and conceptual classification therefore work together.

9. Create a Controlled Vocabulary

For important systems, terminology should become a governed artifact rather than an accidental outcome of documentation.

A simple vocabulary registry could contain:

Semantic Anchor Meaning Type Avoid Using
AstraHub AI orchestration environment Platform AI Platform, Platform
DataHarbor Governed enterprise data repository Data Domain Data Platform, Data Store
SpecScout Requirement-analysis agent Agent Requirement Agent
PolicyLens Policy evaluation capability Service Policy Engine
FlowGate Transaction approval workflow Process Approval Process

AI systems should receive this vocabulary as part of their persistent project context.

Humans should use the same vocabulary in requirements, architecture, development, testing, and operations.

Adopting it without a programme

  • Start with one bounded context, not the whole enterprise. Ambiguity is local first.
  • Keep the registry as a versioned file in the repository, next to the API contracts.
  • Enforce two rules only: first mention is identity + description, and two concepts never share an identity.
  • Review the registry when the architecture changes — a stale anchor is worse than a generic noun.
  • Feed the registry into every agent's persistent context and every retrieval index build.

10. A Simple Test for Choosing a Name

Ask one question:

If this sentence were removed from its document and shown independently to an AI or a new team member six months later, would they still know exactly what it refers to?

If the answer is no, the terminology probably depends too heavily on surrounding context.

Compare:

"The platform sends the validated request to the service."

with:

"AstraHub sends the validated request to PolicyLens."

The second sentence requires significantly less reconstructed context.

That becomes increasingly valuable when thousands of pieces of enterprise knowledge are being chunked, embedded, retrieved, summarized, and recombined by AI.

11. Language Is Becoming Part of Architecture

In traditional software engineering, we carefully designed:

  • APIs
  • database schemas
  • class names
  • namespaces
  • message formats
  • domain models

In AI-assisted engineering, we must add another artifact: the system vocabulary.

Names are no longer simply labels for human convenience.

They influence retrieval.

They influence context generation.

They influence how AI distinguishes concepts.

They influence how agents communicate.

They influence whether knowledge retains its meaning when extracted from its original source.

Therefore, terminology should increasingly be treated with the same discipline as API contracts and data models.

12. What the Research Already Says

The argument above is often made as a matter of style. It is actually the intersection of several well-studied phenomena, each of which independently predicts the same outcome.

Established concept What it establishes Consequence for naming
Information content & inverse document frequency (Shannon, 1948; Robertson & Spärck Jones, BM25, 1976) A symbol's information is −log p; in retrieval, a term is weighted by how rare it is across the corpus. A word present in every platform document is down-weighted exactly where you need it to discriminate. See the calculation below.
Zipf's law and the principle of least effort (Zipf, 1949) Both writers and readers drift toward the shortest high-frequency form available. "The platform" and "the service" are not carelessness; they are the predictable steady state of an ungoverned vocabulary.
The vocabulary problem (Furnas et al., CACM, 1987) Unconstrained people choose the same word for the same object only 10–20% of the time. Agreement cannot be assumed, so terminology must be legislated, not consensus-built.
Polysemy and lexical ambiguity in information retrieval One surface form, several senses: a single token sequence matches the union of all meanings. "Review process" cannot be disambiguated by ranking; only a distinct form removes the ambiguity.
Hubness and anisotropy in high-dimensional spaces (Radovanović et al., JMLR, 2010; Ethayarajh, 2019) In high dimensions a few points become "hubs" that are near neighbours of almost everything; contextual embeddings occupy a narrow cone, not a sphere. Generic concept vectors sit in the crowded centre and match everything weakly. A distinctive anchor occupies sparse space and matches precisely.
Encoding specificity and context-dependent memory (Tulving & Thomson, 1973; Godden & Baddeley, 1975) Recall succeeds when the cue at retrieval matches the cue at encoding. An anchor is context folded into the cue. The chunk carries its own retrieval context, so it still works when re-embedded alone.
Distinctiveness (von Restorff) effect, 1933 An item that differs from its neighbours is disproportionately remembered and recalled. Distinctive names also help humans scanning hundreds of near-identical tickets, tests and dashboards.
Identifier quality in program comprehension (Lawrie, Feild & Binkley, 2006; Hofmeister et al., 2017) Intention-revealing, full-word identifiers improve comprehension; abbreviations measurably increase reading time. The anchor must be a pronounceable word, not a compressed code. Distinctive ≠ cryptic.
Ubiquitous Language and Bounded Context (Evans, Domain-Driven Design, 2003) A term is unambiguous only inside its boundary; the same word legitimately differs across boundaries. "Platform" is not a bad word. It is a word used outside its boundary without the boundary being present in the text.
Unique Name Assumption (description logics, OWL) Distinct names denote distinct individuals unless equality is explicitly asserted. The formal version of "one concept, one name" — and the reason duplicate names silently merge two entities in a knowledge graph.
Controlled vocabulary standards (SKOS; ISO 25964 thesauri) Preferred term, non-preferred term ("use for"), scope note. The registry in section 9 is a known, standard artifact type, not an invention.
Subword tokenization (BPE; Sennrich et al., 2016) Rare strings are split into more subword tokens. The one genuine cost of coining: keep anchors short, pronounceable and ideally two syllables.

The retrieval argument in one calculation

Take the BM25 inverse-document-frequency term, ln((N − df + 0.5) / (df + 0.5) + 1), over a corpus of 10,000 documents. A term that appears in 2,000 of them scores about 1.6; a term that appears in 3 of them scores about 8.0. The generic word is worth roughly one fifth of the distinctive one, in the only currency lexical retrieval uses. No amount of ranking sophistication recovers a distinction the vocabulary never made.

Why the convergence matters. Four independent mechanisms — information weight in lexical retrieval, geometry in dense retrieval, cue-matching in human memory, and readability in program comprehension — all point to the same design rule. When unrelated lines of evidence agree, the rule stops being a matter of taste and starts being engineering.

13. Where This Rule Does Not Apply

Like every naming discipline, it has boundaries:

  • External contracts. Never rename a published API field, event or message to coin an anchor. Anchor the documentation, not the wire format.
  • One anchor per concept. Synonyms defeat the purpose — that is what the "Avoid Using" column in the registry exists for.
  • Ordinary prose. Marketing copy and narrative text need rhythm, not a registry.
  • Near-identical anchors. If two anchors differ by a letter (RiskProbe / RiskPro), you have created a new collision. Prefer different first syllables.
  • Token cost. A coined name can cost more tokens than a common word in models that use subword tokenization. Two short syllables keep the price negligible.
  • Onboarding. An anchor is only cheap if it is defined once and easy to find — the description must always travel with the first mention.

Conclusion: Design for Shared Human-AI Understanding

Enterprise systems are entering an environment where humans and AI continuously consume the same knowledge.

That changes the requirements for language.

We need terminology that is:

Distinctive. Stable. Searchable. Consistent. Context-independent.

For the important concepts within a system, a deliberately uncommon word can provide something extremely valuable: identity.

The principle can therefore be summarized simply:

Give common concepts descriptions. Give important concepts identities.

When a concept has an identity that survives requirements, architecture, code, tests, documentation, RAG retrieval, and AI conversations, we reduce ambiguity for both humans and machines.

In an AI-native world, designing the vocabulary may become as important as designing the system itself.
